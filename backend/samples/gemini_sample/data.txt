Constructing Inpatient Pressure Injury Prediction Models
Using Machine Learning Techniques
Ya-Han Hu, PhD, Yi-Lien Lee, Ming-Feng Kang, MS, Pei-Ju Lee, PhD
The incidence rate of pressure injury is a critical nursing
quality indicator inclinic care; consequently, factors causing
pressure injury are diverse and complex. The early preven
tion of pressure injury and monitoring of these complex
high-risk factors are critical to reducethepatients' pain, pre
vent further surgical treatment, avoid prolonged hospital
stay, decreasethe riskof woundinfection, andlower associ
atedmedicalcostsandexpenses.Althoughanumberofrisk
assessmentscalesofpressureinjury have been adopted
in various countries, their criteria are set for specific pop
ulations, which may not be suitable for the medical care
systems of other countries. This study constructs three
prediction models of inpatient pressure injury using ma
chine learning techniques, including decision tree, logistic
regression, and random forest. A total of 11 838 inpatient
records were collected, and 30 sets of training samples
were adopted for data analysis in the experiment. The ex
perimental results and evaluations of the models suggest
that the prediction model built using random forest had most
favorable classification performance of 0.845. The critical
risk factors for pressure injury identified in this study were
skin integrity, systolic blood pressure, expression ability, cap
illary refill time, and level of consciousness.

Braden Scale5 is the most commonly used clinical tool for
pressure injury prediction as a result of its high sensitivity
and specificity. However, previous studies have shown that
some risk factors for pressure injury, such as sex, length of
hospital stay, disease severity, medication use, diagnosis, and
nursing workload, are not taken into consideration within
the Braden Scale.5
The pressure injury assessment scales currently used by
hospitals in Taiwan for the systematic checkup of patients
prior to hospital admission are derived from the pressure in

has been no single universal pressure injury assessment scale
developed for Taiwan. The Joint Commission of Taiwan
provides hospitals with various healthcare quality indicators
and training programs to improve healthcare for patients.

suggests that some risk factors are not included in the clinical
pressure injury assessment scales. Furthermore, the predic

ture of the risk factors for pressure injury. Therefore, the es

Because of the nursing human-resource shortage and
heavy workload, acomprehensivepressureinjury assessment

patients. After identification, preventive care can be pro

Raff9 conducted a study using logistic regression (LGR)
andfoundthefollowingrisk factorsfor pressure injury: spinal
cord injury, mechanical ventilation, and age of 40 years or
older. Some demographic information such as age, hospital de
partment, and sex were included in many previous studies.10–16
Raju et al17 used LGR, decision tree (DT), and random
forest (RF) to examine risk factors for general inpatient pres
sure injury and ranked them in the following order accord
ing to their level of significance: length of hospital stay, serum
albumin, age, blood urine nitrogen, and total Braden score.
Lyder et al18 analyzed retrospective data on pressure injury
and showed that age, cancer, congestive heart failure, chronic
obstructive pulmonary disease, cardiovascular diseases, diabe
tes, obesity, rehospitalization, and prolonged length of hospi
tal stay were all significantly correlated with pressure injury.
Using multivariate linear regression, Cox19 and Cremasco
et al20 analyzed the development of pressure injury in pa
tients in the intensive care unit and identified the following
risk factors for pressure injury: sex, age, length of hospital
stay, mobility, frictional/shear forces, adrenaline infusion,
cardiovascular diseases, disease severity, and nursing work
load. In addition to Cox,19 the length of hospital stay and
medical history11,14,15,18,21 and the level of mobility5,10,11,22,23
were also commonly considered.
Lahmann et al24,25 used the χ2 automatic interaction de
tection to model a classification tree for identifying the pre
dictors of pressure injury and showed that friction and shear
are the most potent predictors of pressure injury. Okuwa
et al26 used LGR to analyze risk factors for pressure injury
in the lower limbs and reported that the key risk factors were
length of bed rest, male sex, lower limb contracture, toe con
tracture, ankle-brachial index, hemoglobin, red blood cells,
sodium ions, and chloride ions. Schoonhoven et al27 ana
lyzed the independent predictors of pressure injury by using
LGR and found that the predictors were age, body weight
at admission, abnormal appearance of the skin, friction
and shear, and planned surgery.28 Skin appearance and
properties were considered as one major indicator of
pressure injury.3,4,10,13–16,22,25,29
This study used classification techniques to establish a
domestic inpatient pressure injury prediction model intended
to assist nurses to identify patients at high risk. This model au
tomatically screens and identifies patients who are at a po
tential high risk of pressure injury using the electronic medical
record, thereby simplifying the current complex process that

MATERIALS
Data Collection
This study was conducted using clinical data from one re
gional teaching hospital in Taiwan; it is a metropolitan hos
pital with 455 beds. The study applied for access to patients'
electronic medical records and related information from the
institutional review board of the hospital. The data were col
lected from patients hospitalized between the years 2014 and
2016. Each admission of a patient at a different time was con
sidered as a single data point and all data were deidentified
in the analysis. Patients' medical information, including nurs
ing assessment at admission, laboratory test results, and pres
sure injury-related information, were collected retrospectively.
When the data values were missing, the authors performed
value imputation based on the similarity between samples
and deleted samples if too many values were missing; an
excess of missing values may lead to analysis errors. The lit
erature shows that the causes of pressure injury differ sig
nificantly between adults and children.30 Therefore, the
present study targeted adults and excluded patients younger
than 18 years.
Variables
In Taiwan, domestic pressure injury assessment scales are
mostly derived from the Braden Scale,5 Gosnell Scale,23
WaterlowScale,29andNortonScale.31Thesescalesare used
by different hospitals, and the assessment criteria are set up
differently according to the target population as well as ap
plicability and feasibility in terms of medical care systems.
The contents of these four pressure injury assessments are
different, and the criteria for determining the risk of pressure
injury vary. To facilitate clinical use, these measurements do
not include all variables that may affect pressure injury;
therefore, using these measurements is not enough.
According to previous studies, risk factors for pressure
injury can be divided into internal and external factors.
The internal factors include patients' health problems or
conditions, which are sex, age, malnutrition, albumin level,
anemia, hypotension, incontinence, decreased level of con
sciousness in daily activities, systemic infection, chronic dis
eases, disease severity, smoking habit, increases in physical
and mental stress, and negative emotions. The external fac
tors are the amount of pressure imposed on tissues, length of
stay, shear force, friction, and high humidity.1,21,32,33

The Barthel Index represents the activities of daily living
assessment scale, which is the most commonly used scale to
assess physical function in the case of long-term care and
home care of the National Health Insurance. The scoring
items in the Barthel Index include the ability to feed, move
from wheelchair to bed and return, personal toilet, get on
and off toilet, bathe self, walk on level surface, ascend and
descend stairs, dress, and control bowels and bladder. A
higher total score on the Barthel Index means the patient is
less dependent on assistance. The total score on the Barthel
Index and the incidence of pressure injury were negatively
correlated; this implies a higher likelihood of patients being
bedridden, which in turn can increase the rate of pressure in
jury because of prolonged pressure on the skin.

existing scales, and clinical recommendations, this study se

DATA ANALYSIS
After data collection and preprocessing, a total of 11 677
non–pressure injury cases and 161 pressure injury cases were
included inthe data analysis. The data set was randomlysplit
into training and validation sets (1:1 ratio). That is, 50% of
the data were used to train the best classifier in the tests
and the remaining 50% of the data were used to validate
the model performance. Toselect an optimal feature set that
contains the independent variables highly correlated with
the dependent variable (class) but uncorrelated with each
other, a correlation-based feature selection technique, the
CfsSubsetEval module of the data mining software Waikato
Environmentfor KnowledgeAnalysis(WEKA;Universityof
Waikato, New Zealand), was applied.
In the collected dataset, the rate of pressure injury was
1.36%,suggesting animbalancedratio betweenthetwo class
labels. To avoid the class imbalance problem, the resample
module of WEKA was employed to both undersample the
majority class and oversample the minority class by the boot
strap method. As a result, the injury and non–pressure injury
cases were adjusted at a 1:1 ratio in the training set. More
over, some useful samples in both classes may not be chosen
by the resample module, resulting in a possible bias in the
evaluations of model performance. Therefore, the bootstrap
method was applied 10 times to construct 10 training datasets;
for each generated training dataset, 10-fold cross-validation
was then conducted in all the experimental evaluations.35
In this study, WEKA was used to build prediction models
of DT,LGR,andRF.Logisticregression issimilar to the lin
ear regression analysis that mainly explores the relationship
between the dependent variable and the independent vari
able. The dependent variable in linear regression is usually
a continuous variable; however, in this case, the dependent
variable discussed by LGR is mainly a categorical variable,
especially a variable divided into two categories. In addition
to using a binary dependent variable, there are other exten
sions for more thantwolevelsofdependentvariables, includ
ing multinomial logistic regression or ordinal LGR. Decision
tree uses the concept of tree branching as a decision-making
model, which can be applied to classification and regression.
Because the prediction result can display not only the category
type but also the numerical data, the DT can be expanded
into the classification tree, regression tree, or classification
andregression tree. Random forest can be seen as an ensem
ble learning model of multiple DTs. Similar to the DT, the
RF can be applied to classification and regression. When a
new target is classified by RF, each tree in the RF will give
its own classification label and vote for it. The overall output
of the forest will be the classification option with the most
votes and the average of all DT outputs in the regression
problem. The DT, the LGR, and the RF models were con
structed using the J48, SimpleLogistic, and RandomForest
modulesinWEKA,respectively.Becausetheprediction per
formance of classifiers can be significantly influenced by the
hyperparameter settings, the CVParameterSelection meta
learner module of WEKA was used to optimize the hyper
parameters for each classifier. Table 2 shows the details of
the parameter tuning for each classification technique.
After the best classifier is determined from the test of train
ing sets, the best classifier is then used with optimal parameter
settings to examine the performance using validation set.
Theconfusion matrix was adopted to evaluate the perfor
mance of each prediction model (Figure 1). True-positive
(TP) is the number of inpatient pressure injuries in the sam
ple and predicted to be successful; true-negative (TN) is the
number of inpatient non–pressure injuries in the sample
and predicted to be inpatient without pressure injury; false
positive (FP) is the number of inpatient pressure injuries that
wereactually hospitalized for non–pressure injury patients in
the sample; and false-negative (FN) is the number of inpa
tient non–pressure injuries that were actually hospitalized
for pressure injury in the sample.
